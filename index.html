Understanding how cancer cells acquire the capacity to evolve

I am an experimental cancer biologist studying how genome instability and dynamic genome architecture generate cell-state plasticity and therapeutic resistance. My long-term goal is to define the mechanisms that connect genome evolution to cancer cell identity and to identify strategies that constrain therapeutic escape.

Research vision

From genome plasticity to cell-state evolution

Cancer evolution is not simply the accumulation of mutations. I am interested in how changes in genome sequence, structure, and chromatin organization alter the repertoire of cellular states that tumors can access under selective pressure. Using advanced prostate cancer as a primary experimental system, my work asks how genomic diversity is generated, how it reshapes cell identity, and whether the capacity to evolve can itself become a therapeutic vulnerability.

Independent research program

Three questions shaping my future laboratory

01

Origins of Genome Plasticity

How does genome instability generate the diversity required for cancer evolution?

We investigate how endogenous mutational processes, including APOBEC activity and DNA damage, generate genetic and structural genome diversity, with a particular interest in the mechanisms that promote ecDNA formation.

APOBECDNA damageecDNA biogenesis
02

Genome-to-State Evolution

How does genome architecture determine which cellular states a cancer can access?

We test how dynamic genome structures, particularly ecDNA, reshape transcriptional programs, heterogeneity, and lineage identity, using experimentally controlled systems to distinguish causality from association.

ecDNAcell statelineage plasticity
03

Intercepting Cancer Evolution

Can the evolutionary capacity of cancer cells be therapeutically constrained?

We identify mechanisms and vulnerabilities that limit genomic diversification, stabilize cell identity, or selectively eliminate highly plastic states, linking fundamental cancer evolution to new therapeutic strategies.

Scientific trajectory

Key contributions

2020

Chromatin plasticity enables heterogeneous resistance trajectories

Helped establish that loss of the chromatin remodeler CHD1 creates a permissive chromatin state that enables prostate cancer cells to access multiple alternative programs under AR-targeted therapy.

Cancer Cell · co-first author

2023

APOBEC mutagenesis accelerates therapeutic evolution

Discovered that loss of SYNCRIP unleashes APOBEC3B-mediated mutagenesis, increases tumor heterogeneity, and accelerates the emergence of resistant clones during AR-targeted therapy.

Cancer Cell · first author

Future

Connecting genome architecture to cancer cell identity

My independent program extends these discoveries to ask how structural genome plasticity, especially ecDNA, couples genomic evolution to cell-state plasticity and therapeutic adaptation.

About

Experimental cancer biology with an evolutionary perspective

I am an Associate Research Scientist in the Department of Urology at Yale School of Medicine. My research focuses on the mechanisms by which cancer cells evolve under therapeutic pressure, with particular interests in genome instability, cell-state plasticity, and advanced prostate cancer.

I received my Ph.D. in Cell Biology from Zhejiang University and completed postdoctoral training in cancer biology at UT Southwestern Medical Center. My long-term goal is to build an independent research program that combines mechanistic experimentation with functional genomics to understand and ultimately constrain cancer evolution.
