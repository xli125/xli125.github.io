  <section class="faculty-section vision-section">
    <div class="section-eyebrow">Research vision</div>
    <h2>From genome plasticity to cell-state evolution</h2>

    <p class="section-intro">
      Cancer evolution is not simply the accumulation of mutations. I am interested in how changes in
      genome sequence, structure, and chromatin organization alter the repertoire of cellular states that
      tumors can access under selective pressure. Using advanced prostate cancer as a primary experimental
      system, my work asks how genomic diversity is generated, how it reshapes cell identity, and whether
      the capacity to evolve can itself become a therapeutic vulnerability.
    </p>

  <section class="faculty-section">
    <div class="section-eyebrow">Independent research program</div>
    <h2>Three questions shaping my future laboratory</h2>

    <div class="research-grid">
      <article class="research-card">
        <div class="card-index">01</div>
        <h3>Origins of Genome Plasticity</h3>
        <p class="research-question">How does genome instability generate the diversity required for cancer evolution?</p>
        <p>
          We investigate how endogenous mutational processes, including APOBEC activity and DNA damage,
          generate genetic and structural genome diversity, with a particular interest in the mechanisms
          that promote ecDNA formation.
        </p>
        <div class="topic-tags">
          <span>APOBEC</span><span>DNA damage</span><span>ecDNA biogenesis</span>
        </div>
      </article>

      <article class="research-card">
        <div class="card-index">02</div>
        <h3>Genome-to-State Evolution</h3>
        <p class="research-question">How does genome architecture determine which cellular states a cancer can access?</p>
        <p>
          We test how dynamic genome structures, particularly ecDNA, reshape transcriptional programs,
          heterogeneity, and lineage identity, using experimentally controlled systems to distinguish
          causality from association.
        </p>
        <div class="topic-tags">
          <span>ecDNA</span><span>cell state</span><span>lineage plasticity</span>
        </div>
      </article>

      <article class="research-card">
        <div class="card-index">03</div>
        <h3>Intercepting Cancer Evolution</h3>
        <p class="research-question">Can the evolutionary capacity of cancer cells be therapeutically constrained?</p>
        <p>
          We identify mechanisms and vulnerabilities that limit genomic diversification, stabilize cell
          identity, or selectively eliminate highly plastic states, linking fundamental cancer evolution
          to new therapeutic strategies.
        </p>
        <div class="topic-tags">
          <span>therapy resistance</span><span>chemical biology</span><span>evolvability</span>
        </div>
      </article>
    </div>
  </section>

    <h2>Key contributions</h2>

    <div class="contribution-list">
      <article class="contribution-item">
        <div class="contribution-year">2020</div>

          <h3>Chromatin plasticity enables heterogeneous resistance trajectories</h3>
          <p>

            AR-targeted therapy.

        <div>
          <h3>APOBEC mutagenesis accelerates therapeutic evolution</h3>
          <p>
            Discovered that loss of SYNCRIP unleashes APOBEC3B-mediated mutagenesis, increases tumor
            heterogeneity, and accelerates the emergence of resistant clones during AR-targeted therapy.
          </p>

          </p>
          <p class="journal-line">Independent research vision</p>

      <h2>Experimental cancer biology with an evolutionary perspective</h2>
    </div>

    <div class="about-columns">
      <p>
        I am an Associate Research Scientist in the Department of Urology at Yale School of Medicine.
        My research focuses on the mechanisms by which cancer cells evolve under therapeutic pressure,
        with particular interests in genome instability, cell-state plasticity, and advanced prostate cancer.
      </p>
      <p>
        I received my Ph.D. in Cell Biology from Zhejiang University and completed postdoctoral training
        in cancer biology at UT Southwestern Medical Center. My long-term goal is to build an independent
        research program that combines mechanistic experimentation with functional genomics to understand
        and ultimately constrain cancer evolution.
      </p>
